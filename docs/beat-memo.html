The Beat Memo

What Beat is for, why the shape of the day matters more than the shape of the clock, and what happens when the planner starts talking back.

Written at beat 2/100, day 58/100, 2026 — or, if you insist, sometime around 11pm on a Monday in late August.

Beat replaces the question “what time is it?” with a better one: “how much of today is left?” Every day is exactly 100 beats. A beat is 14 minutes and 24 seconds — short enough to feel like a unit of actual work, long enough that you never have to think in seconds. When someone asks you the time, you don’t say 3:47pm. You say beat 26, and everyone in the room understands exactly how much runway is left before the day is spent.

The mechanism

The trick is which end of the ladder is fixed. Most attempts at decimal time start from the year and divide downward, which sounds tidy until you realize a year never splits evenly into anything — so the fudge factor lands wherever you least want it, usually right in the middle of your afternoon. Beat starts from the one unit that is fixed: the calendar day, DST and all. Everything below it nests by a clean hundredfold — mote (86.4ms) → grain (8.64s) → beat (14m 24s) — and everything above it does too — day → span (100 days). The one seam in the whole system sits at the very top, where a year comes out to roughly 3.65 spans instead of a clean 100. That seam is a hundred-day span short, once a year, as far from your afternoon as the math can put it.

The payoff is that “100 beats left today” is never an approximation. It’s not a progress bar rounding for effect — it’s exact, the same way “60 minutes in an hour” is exact. That's what makes it usable as a real unit of account, not just a novelty readout.

Who actually wants this

Clock time answers a question almost nobody is asking. You rarely need to know your position in the day — you need to know how much of it you have left. Beat is for the specific moment that happens a dozen times a day: glancing up mid-task and needing an honest answer to “do I have time to start something else, or should I wind down?” A beat count answers that in one glance. A clock face makes you do the subtraction yourself, every single time.

Three groups get the most out of it in practice:

People who plan their day in blocks, not appointments. The Day view turns beats into a 10×10 grid you tap out directly — deep work, meetings, the gym — at a grain (14.4 minutes) that’s coarse enough to plan quickly and fine enough to mean something, which is a gap most calendars leave between “15-minute slots” and “just wing it.”

People who lose the thread of a clock-shaped day. A depleting count is a fundamentally different kind of information than a clock position — it tells you a quantity, not a location, and quantities are easier to feel in your body. That’s the whole premise behind the beat and grain grids pulsing on the Now screen: the current cell isn’t just numerically current, it’s visibly current.

People who want the day to hold them accountable back. Blocks aren’t just decoration on the grid — when the live beat crosses into one, Beat surfaces it; when a block ends, it asks whether you actually did the thing. It’s a planner that checks in, not a calendar that just sits there being ignored.

Show date: ON

None of this asks you to abandon clock time. The date toggle is deliberate — the goal is fluency, not conversion. You keep the bridge (10:49pm, right next to beat 5/100) for as long as you need it, and hide it the day you don’t.

Where it goes from here

The core arithmetic is done and tested; almost everything left is about making the plan you make in Beat easier to make well, and easier to trust.

Near

Recurring templates. Save a day’s block layout — the usual sleep, the usual focus window — and start from it instead of an empty grid every morning.

Calendar import. Pull real events in and snap them to the nearest beat boundary automatically, so Beat and your existing calendar stop being two separate chores.

A completion record. Every Done / Not done / Snooze already writes a status. Surfacing that history — which kinds of blocks you actually finish, which you quietly always skip — turns the planner into a mirror.

Mid

A menu-bar or Waybar widget. The full screen is for planning; a status bar is for glancing. A live beat 26 sitting where the clock usually lives is the most honest version of the pitch, and Omarchy’s Waybar is about as natural a home for that as exists.

Sync across devices. Blocks currently live in one browser’s local storage. A day planned on a laptop should be the same day a phone can check.

Far

Shared beats. A block two people can both see — “we’re both heads-down until beat 40” — without either of them opening a shared calendar.

An event hook. Let a block starting or ending trigger something outside Beat — a focus mode, a do-not-disturb toggle — instead of asking other tools to poll a calendar.

What the AI version looks like

The tempting version of “add AI to a planner” is a chatbot bolted onto the side. The useful version is narrower and more specific: an assistant whose only job is translating between how you actually talk about your day and the beat grid underneath it — in both directions.

Concretely, three things:

Planning in sentences, not taps. You describe the day once, in your own words, and it comes back as blocks already placed on the grid:

2 hours deep work this morning, a call with Sam at 3, gym after dinner beats 5–13 · Deep work beats 71–74 · Call: Sam beats 88–94 · Gym

A time-estimation coach that learns from you, specifically. Beat already knows the gap between what you planned and what you marked done. An assistant reading that history stops treating every “deep work” block as a fresh guess — it notices that yours run eight beats, not the twelve you keep scheduling, and quietly proposes the number that actually holds.

Prompts that know the pattern, not just the moment. A static “did you get this done?” is the same question whether it’s your first skipped gym block or your tenth. One that’s read the history can ask the more useful version — “this is the third evening gym block in a row you’ve skipped — move it to mornings instead?” — and act on the answer.

None of that requires the assistant to know anything it wasn’t already sitting on — the block history, the done/skipped record, the grid itself. The bar for whether an AI feature belongs in Beat is whether it makes the beats you already track more useful, not whether it gives the app something new to talk about. And it stays a proposal, never a silent edit: the grid is yours, the assistant only ever suggests what goes on it.

Everything above the AI section already exists and ships today. Everything in it is a direction, not a feature list — worth building in roughly the order it’s written, starting wherever the next real day of use points.
